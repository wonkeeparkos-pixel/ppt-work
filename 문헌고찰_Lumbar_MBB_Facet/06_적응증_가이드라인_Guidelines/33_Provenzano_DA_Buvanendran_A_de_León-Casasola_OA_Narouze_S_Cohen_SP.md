### [미확인] The MINT critiques cluster into three arguments — patient selection, RF technique, and co-intervention dilution — and the formal published rebuttal is Provenzano et al., Reg Anesth Pain Med 2018.

UNVERIFIED IN-SESSION. Recalled rebuttal citation: Provenzano DA, Buvanendran A, de León-Casasola OA, Narouze S, Cohen SP. 'Interpreting the MINT Randomized Trials Evaluating Radiofrequency Ablation for Axial Low Back Pain...' Reg Anesth Pain Med 2018;43(1):68-71 (PMID recalled 29280928 — verify title and PMID). The three lines of attack: (1) SELECTION — MINT used a single diagnostic block with a >=50% relief threshold rather than dual comparative blocks at >=80%; given a single-block false-positive rate around 38% (Schwarzer, Pain 1994), a substantial fraction of randomized patients plausibly did not have facet-mediated pain at all, biasing toward the null. (2) TECHNIQUE — the most substantive criticism: electrode placement was argued to deviate from SIS-recommended technique, in particular insufficiently parallel/tangential alignment of the active tip to the medial branch and inadequate lesion size or number, so the nerve may not have been reliably coagulated. If the nerve was not lesioned, the trial tested the operator, not the procedure. (3) DESIGN — every arm received a standardized exercise program, and the comparator's effect plus regression to the mean can compress a real between-group difference; pragmatic multi-center delivery adds operator heterogeneity. Additional critical correspondence appeared in JAMA (recalled: a letter by Vorobeychik, Stojanovic, McCormick, JAMA 2017;318(22) — verify) and in the interventional pain literature. Counter-position to present fairly: MINT's defenders argue these are post-hoc rescue arguments, that MINT reflects real-world practice, and that demanding ideal selection and technique concedes the procedure's effectiveness is fragile.
